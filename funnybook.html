The Neverminded Protocol
Chapter 1: The Printer Situation

The pharmacy printer had chosen violence.

It had not exploded, caught fire, or even made a particularly dramatic noise. Instead it had simply stopped cooperating with reality. The screen displayed a message that read Driver Error: Unknown Device, which was impressive considering the device in question had been sitting in the same place for four years and had printed approximately seventy thousand prescription labels without questioning its own identity.

Anzel van der Berg stared at the machine the way a general studies a battlefield that has suddenly betrayed him.

“This printer,” she said slowly, “is testing my patience.”

Across the counter Vernon leaned over the workstation with a screwdriver in one hand and a USB cable draped over his shoulder like a piece of climbing gear. His expression suggested that he had already entered the deep and unpleasant philosophical territory of modern hardware diagnostics.

“Printers,” he said calmly, “are the closest thing technology has to a personal vendetta.”

The pharmacy smelled faintly of antiseptic, floor polish, and the medicinal sweetness of cough syrup. Shelves of medication stretched along the walls in precise rows that reflected Anzel’s particular brand of organizational discipline. Nothing in the store was out of place except the printer, which had apparently decided that society itself was optional.

A customer approached the counter holding a box of Neurolift.

“Excuse me,” the man said pleasantly, “I think the dosage instructions might be incorrect.”

Anzel turned with professional reflex. “What seems to be the problem?”

“Well,” the man explained with a cheerful shrug, “my house caught fire this morning and I watched the whole thing burn down while eating cereal. It was actually quite fascinating. My wife thought I should maybe ask about side effects.”

Anzel blinked once.

Vernon slowly lifted his head from the computer.

“That… seems like a fairly significant side effect.”

The man laughed in a relaxed and almost musical way. “Oh it’s probably nothing. I’ve just been very calm lately.”

Anzel studied him carefully. He did not look distressed. He did not look traumatized. He looked like a man describing an interesting documentary.

She glanced down at the box in his hand.

Neurolift.

The product had arrived two weeks earlier as part of a promotional campaign. Half-price launch discount. Stress reduction supplement. Completely legal. Mild neurological stabilizer.

Sales had exploded almost immediately.

People loved it.

“Did you call the fire department?” she asked.

“Oh yes,” the man replied. “Eventually. After the roof collapsed.”

Vernon rubbed his face.

“Sir,” he said gently, “most people would consider that an emergency.”

The man tilted his head thoughtfully.

“That does sound reasonable now that you mention it.”

Anzel printed his receipt manually using the backup label system while the main printer continued sulking beside the computer like an appliance that had recently read existential philosophy.

When the customer left, Vernon leaned back in the chair.

“That guy just described watching his house burn down like it was a nature documentary.”

“Stress reactions can be strange,” Anzel said.

“Not that strange.”

Another customer entered the store, moving slowly but smiling broadly.

An elderly woman approached the counter holding a Neurolift bottle.

“My husband crashed the car into our garage,” she said pleasantly. “We’re both perfectly fine but he’s been sitting in the driveway staring at the smoke for about twenty minutes. I thought perhaps the dosage is too strong.”

Vernon turned his chair slightly.

“You’re telling me your husband drove into your own house.”

“Yes.”

“And he’s just… admiring the smoke?”

“It really is quite pretty.”

Anzel felt a small, uncomfortable sensation forming somewhere near the back of her thoughts.

Coincidence was a reasonable explanation.

Two coincidences in the same afternoon was less comforting.

She handed the woman a revised dosage instruction and watched her leave with the same peaceful smile.

Vernon had already returned his attention to the computer.

“Alright,” he muttered, “let’s see what this stupid thing is hiding.”

The printer driver window expanded across the screen, followed by several system prompts that did not belong to any normal printer installation.

Vernon frowned.

“That’s odd.”

“What is?”

“This driver isn’t local.”

He opened another diagnostic panel and watched several network connections appear automatically.

“Your pharmacy software is linked to a remote system.”

“That’s normal,” Anzel said. “Prescription tracking.”

Vernon shook his head slowly.

“No. This isn’t medical regulation infrastructure. This is something else.”

He clicked deeper into the system and discovered a hidden database connection labeled HELIX BIOMEDICAL MONITORING NODE.

“Well,” Vernon said thoughtfully, “that seems suspicious.”

Anzel leaned over the counter.

“Why is a biomedical company connected to my printer?”

“Technically,” Vernon replied, “it’s connected to your entire sales system.”

The screen flickered as Vernon opened the data stream.

Patient behavior logs.

Purchase patterns.

Psychological response notes.

Every Neurolift purchase from the pharmacy was being uploaded automatically.

Anzel stared at the screen.

“That cannot be legal.”

Vernon had already begun exploring the system with the dangerous curiosity of a man who had forgotten the concept of consequences.

“Oh it gets better.”

Another window opened.

Research documentation.

Neurological response graphs.

Behavioral compliance modeling.

Vernon slowly leaned closer to the monitor.

“Anzel,” he said quietly, “this drug doesn’t reduce stress.”

“What does it do?”

He opened a clinical trial summary.

“It suppresses the brain’s fear response.”

Anzel crossed her arms.

“That sounds… medically irresponsible.”

“Not just irresponsible.”

He scrolled further.

The research notes described emotional dampening, risk indifference, and neurological compliance patterns.

People remained conscious.

Functional.

Productive.

But completely detached from danger.

Anzel felt the uncomfortable sensation in her mind grow sharper.

“You mean it removes panic.”

“Yes.”

“And urgency.”

“Yes.”

“And survival instincts.”

Vernon looked up from the screen.

“Pretty much.”

They sat in silence for a moment.

Outside the pharmacy window a car rolled slowly down the street with no driver visible behind the wheel. It bumped gently into a parked vehicle and stopped.

The driver climbed out calmly and examined the damage with mild curiosity.

“Well,” the man said to no one in particular, “that was interesting.”

Inside the pharmacy Vernon slowly turned back to the computer.

“I think,” he said carefully, “we may have discovered something extremely illegal.”

The screen flashed.

Several new connections appeared simultaneously.

Remote access detected.

Unauthorized system activity flagged.

Vernon sighed.

“Ah.”

Anzel crossed her arms.

“What does ‘ah’ mean?”

“It means,” Vernon said calmly, “someone just noticed we’re here.”

Outside the pharmacy two black SUVs turned into the parking lot.

The Neverminded Protocol
Chapter 2: The Inspection Team

The two black SUVs parked with the quiet confidence of vehicles that had never once been questioned by a parking meter.

Anzel stood behind the pharmacy counter with her arms crossed while Vernon watched the security camera feed on the computer screen. The camera showed four men stepping out of the vehicles in identical dark suits that looked expensive enough to imply either government funding or corporate lawyers who charged by the minute.

“Those are not customers,” Vernon said.

“No,” Anzel replied calmly, “customers usually arrive without tactical posture.”

The men moved with the sort of casual coordination that suggested professional training. One of them paused briefly to scan the building before adjusting his tie as if he were about to attend a business meeting rather than whatever uncomfortable conversation was about to happen inside the pharmacy.

Vernon leaned back in the chair and stared at the screen.

“Well,” he said, “I might have accidentally accessed a database that belongs to a company with a very large legal department.”

“You hacked a biomedical corporation.”

“I prefer the term unplanned system exploration.”

Anzel glanced toward the front door.

“You broke into their system.”

“Yes.”

“You stole their research files.”

“Yes.”

“You triggered their security alerts.”

Vernon nodded slowly.

“That part appears to have happened.”

The front door opened with a cheerful chime that felt wildly inappropriate given the circumstances.

The first man entered the pharmacy with the smooth politeness of someone who had spent years convincing people that unpleasant conversations were actually administrative procedures.

“Good afternoon,” he said pleasantly.

Anzel gave him the same professional smile she used for customers who attempted to pay for prescription medication with expired coupons.

“Welcome to Van der Berg Pharmacy.”

Three more men followed him inside. One remained near the entrance while the others approached the counter with identical expressions of corporate friendliness.

The lead agent produced a badge that looked official enough to discourage casual questions.

“My name is Daniel Cross,” he said. “We’re conducting a regulatory inspection related to pharmaceutical monitoring compliance.”

Vernon watched the man carefully while pretending to adjust a cable behind the computer monitor.

“Inspection,” he murmured quietly.

Anzel did not look at him.

“Of course,” she said politely. “What exactly are you inspecting?”

Cross placed both hands on the counter with relaxed authority.

“Your Neurolift distribution records.”

Vernon slowly leaned forward in his chair.

“Well,” he whispered to Anzel, “that seems like an unfortunate coincidence.”

Anzel kept smiling.

“Our records are perfectly compliant.”

“I’m sure they are,” Cross replied smoothly. “However our company requires verification of certain data streams connected to this location.”

Vernon glanced at the computer screen where the Helix database connection was still blinking like a guilty conscience.

“Your company?” Anzel asked.

“Helix Biomedical.”

There was a moment of silence that felt heavier than it should have.

Cross’s smile widened slightly.

“I believe one of your systems may have recently accessed internal research files that were not intended for public distribution.”

Vernon coughed quietly.

“That sounds like a very unfortunate software malfunction.”

Cross’s eyes shifted toward him.

“And you are?”

“Printer technician.”

Cross nodded slowly.

“Of course you are.”

Another agent stepped forward and placed a small electronic device on the counter. The device displayed a series of network signals that immediately locked onto the pharmacy computer.

“We’re going to need access to that workstation,” Cross said.

Anzel rested her hands calmly on the counter.

“I’m afraid that isn’t possible.”

Cross tilted his head slightly.

“Why not?”

“Because,” Anzel said pleasantly, “this is a pharmacy and you just walked in without a warrant.”

The two agents behind Cross shifted their stance slightly.

The man near the door quietly locked it.

The cheerful entry chime made a small electronic sound as the lock engaged.

Vernon slowly turned his chair.

“That seems unnecessary.”

Cross folded his hands.

“Mr…?”

“Printer technician.”

“Mr Printer Technician, we are attempting to resolve a minor data breach. Cooperation would make this process much simpler.”

Anzel glanced at the locked door.

“You locked my pharmacy.”

“Yes.”

“You brought four men to investigate a printer driver.”

“Yes.”

“And you’re telling me this is a minor issue.”

Cross’s expression did not change.

“Correct.”

Vernon leaned toward Anzel and whispered quietly.

“I may have copied their entire research archive.”

Anzel closed her eyes for one brief moment.

When she opened them again she looked at Cross with the calm patience of someone who had spent fifteen years explaining medication instructions to people who refused to read labels.

“Mr Cross,” she said, “I believe you should leave.”

Cross sighed.

“I was hoping we could avoid complications.”

One of the agents moved toward the counter.

Vernon casually slid a flash drive into his pocket.

Anzel noticed.

Cross noticed.

The room suddenly felt much smaller.

“Sir,” Cross said quietly to Vernon, “please place that drive on the counter.”

Vernon leaned back in his chair.

“I don’t think I will.”

The agent stepped forward.

At the same moment the automated pill dispensing machine beside the workstation began loudly dispensing tablets because Vernon had accidentally leaned on the release button.

A cascade of orange pill bottles erupted onto the floor.

Everyone looked down.

The moment lasted exactly two seconds.

Then Vernon kicked the rolling stool backward into the agent’s legs.

The man stumbled.

Anzel grabbed a tray of syringes from the preparation counter and flung them across the room.

The pharmacy exploded into chaos.

Shelves rattled.

Bottles scattered.

One of the agents slipped on a rolling container of antacids and collided with a display rack of vitamin supplements that collapsed like a nutritional avalanche.

Cross stepped back calmly as pills bounced across the floor.

“You’re making this unnecessarily dramatic,” he said.

Vernon ducked behind the counter while typing rapidly on the keyboard.

“I’m unlocking the back door!”

Another agent pulled a pistol.

Anzel grabbed the nearest object within reach.

It happened to be a large box labeled MAXIMUM STRENGTH LAXATIVE.

She threw it with remarkable force.

The box struck the agent in the face.

He staggered backward and accidentally discharged his weapon into a display of cough syrup bottles.

Glass exploded everywhere.

Vernon looked up from the computer.

“I feel like we’ve crossed an important legal boundary.”

Anzel vaulted over the counter.

“Run.”

The back door burst open as Vernon’s hack disabled the building lock.

They sprinted into the alley behind the pharmacy just as another gunshot shattered the front window.

Vernon slammed the door behind them and kept running.

“Just so we’re clear,” he said breathlessly, “this is officially the worst printer repair job I have ever done.”

Anzel grabbed her car keys while sirens began echoing somewhere in the distance.

“Next time,” she said, climbing into the driver’s seat, “we are buying a new printer.”

Vernon jumped into the passenger seat.

“Agreed.”

The engine roared to life.

Behind them the pharmacy door burst open and Helix agents poured into the alley.

Anzel slammed the accelerator.

The car shot forward into traffic while Vernon clutched the flash drive in his hand.

“Well,” he said.

“What?”

“We just declared war on a biomedical corporation.”

Anzel turned the wheel hard and sped through an intersection.

“Good,” she said.

“Why good?”

“Because now I’m curious.”

The Neverminded Protocol
Chapter 3: Traffic Violations

The pharmacy disappeared behind them as Anzel’s car launched into traffic like a small, determined missile with poor regard for municipal speed regulations.

Vernon gripped the dashboard.

“I feel compelled to mention that you are currently driving through a red light.”

Anzel did not slow down.

“Yes.”

“There was also a pedestrian crossing.”

“Yes.”

“And I believe that mailbox technically counted as property damage.”

“That mailbox,” Anzel said calmly while turning sharply onto the main road, “declared war on my front bumper.”

Behind them the two black SUVs burst out of the alley and joined traffic with the quiet determination of vehicles that had absolutely no intention of respecting traffic laws either.

Vernon twisted around in his seat.

“Oh good,” he said. “They brought friends.”

Anzel checked the mirror.

“How many?”

“Two vehicles.”

“That seems manageable.”

Vernon stared at her.

“You consider armed corporate agents manageable?”

“I consider them extremely irritating.”

She accelerated again, weaving through the afternoon traffic with the precision of someone who had spent years driving between pharmaceutical suppliers on rural roads where speed limits were treated more like philosophical suggestions.

The SUVs gained ground quickly.

“These people are very motivated,” Vernon said.

“That’s because you stole their secret research.”

“I did not steal it.”

“You copied it.”

“I archived it.”

“Without permission.”

“Yes.”

“That’s stealing.”

Vernon sighed.

“I preferred the term archival preservation.”

One of the SUVs swerved around a delivery truck and closed the distance rapidly.

Anzel glanced at the mirror again.

“That one has a driver who knows what he’s doing.”

“Can we outrun them?”

“Eventually.”

“That sounded unconvincing.”

Anzel took the next corner hard enough that Vernon briefly reconsidered several life decisions.

“While you are driving like an unlicensed rally champion,” he said carefully, “I’m going to examine the data that started this whole disaster.”

He pulled the flash drive from his pocket and plugged it into his laptop.

The screen flickered to life.

Several encrypted folders appeared immediately.

“Ah,” Vernon muttered.

“What?”

“They password protected everything.”

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“It’s more of a suggestion than a barrier.”

He began typing rapidly while the car roared down another street.

Behind them the SUVs followed.

Vernon watched the decryption process crawl across the screen.

“This is extremely illegal,” he said thoughtfully.

Anzel turned the wheel sharply to avoid a cyclist.

“You already crossed extremely illegal about twenty minutes ago.”

“That’s fair.”

The first file opened.

Clinical trial reports filled the screen.

Neurological diagrams.

Behavioral graphs.

Vernon leaned closer to the display.

“Interesting.”

“That word usually means something terrible.”

“It means this drug is far more complicated than we thought.”

Another file opened.

Internal corporate presentations.

A slide appeared on the screen.

PROJECT CALM HORIZON

Anzel glanced sideways.

“That name sounds suspiciously like a supervillain project.”

Vernon scrolled.

“It gets better.”

The documents described large-scale behavioral modeling.

Population response patterns.

Urban compliance projections.

Vernon felt his stomach drop slightly.

“This isn’t a consumer medication.”

“What is it?”

“A behavioral control platform.”

Anzel frowned.

“That sounds deeply unethical.”

“It’s worse than unethical.”

He opened another document.

The page displayed a distribution diagram.

Aerosol dispersal systems.

Citywide diffusion models.

Emergency response suppression curves.

Vernon leaned back slowly.

“Oh.”

“What?”

“This drug isn’t meant to be sold.”

Anzel swerved around another car.

“Then why are pharmacies distributing it?”

“Field testing.”

She glanced at him.

“Field testing?”

“Yes.”

“For what?”

He pointed at the screen.

“Mass compliance.”

Silence filled the car for a moment.

Behind them the SUVs were getting closer.

Anzel exhaled slowly.

“You’re telling me this drug removes panic.”

“Yes.”

“And they plan to release it on entire cities.”

“Yes.”

“So riots disappear.”

“Yes.”

“Protests disappear.”

“Yes.”

“People stop resisting authority.”

“Yes.”

Anzel turned another corner and accelerated again.

“That’s not a medication.”

“No.”

“That’s a weapon.”

“Correct.”

The laptop screen suddenly flashed.

A warning message appeared.

REMOTE TRACE DETECTED

Vernon blinked.

“Well that’s unpleasant.”

“What now?”

“They’re tracking the flash drive.”

“From the laptop?”

“Yes.”

“That seems unfair.”

“They built the system.”

Behind them one of the SUVs suddenly accelerated aggressively and rammed the rear of Anzel’s car.

The impact jolted the vehicle forward.

Vernon grabbed the seat.

“Oh good,” he said calmly. “They’ve moved to the murder phase.”

Anzel gripped the wheel and stabilized the car.

“That was rude.”

The SUV rammed them again.

Traffic scattered around them as horns blared.

Vernon looked back through the rear window.

“The man in that vehicle appears extremely committed to his job.”

“That’s because he’s probably paid well.”

“Do you have a plan?”

“Yes.”

“What is it?”

Anzel turned sharply toward the highway entrance ramp.

“We stop running.”

Vernon stared at her.

“I feel like that sentence should have ended differently.”

“We stop running,” she repeated calmly, “and start causing problems.”

The car roared onto the highway.

The SUVs followed.

Vernon looked at the laptop screen where the Helix files continued loading.

“This information could expose their entire operation.”

“Yes.”

“So if we survive,” Vernon said carefully, “we could bring down a biomedical corporation.”

Anzel accelerated again.

“Exactly.”

The highway opened ahead of them.

Behind them the SUVs closed in.

Vernon held the laptop tightly.

“Well,” he said.

“What?”

“This is officially the most exciting printer repair job I have ever done.”

Anzel smiled slightly.

“Wait until the shooting starts.”

The Neverminded Protocol
Chapter 4: Highway Warfare

The highway stretched ahead like a long ribbon of asphalt that had absolutely no idea it was about to become a crime scene.

Anzel pushed the accelerator down another centimeter. The engine responded with a strained growl that suggested the vehicle had not originally been designed for high speed corporate warfare.

Vernon glanced at the speedometer.

“I feel obligated to inform you that we are currently exceeding several legal thresholds.”

Anzel kept her eyes on the road.

“Which ones?”

“Most of them.”

Behind them the two Helix SUVs surged forward through traffic with aggressive precision. One vehicle swerved around a slow-moving truck while the other cut across two lanes in a maneuver that caused several drivers to slam their brakes in alarm.

Vernon twisted around in his seat.

“They appear extremely motivated.”

“That’s because we’re holding their research.”

“That research might expose a neurological control weapon designed for entire populations.”

“Yes.”

“So they probably want it back.”

“Correct.”

Vernon nodded thoughtfully.

“That seems reasonable from their perspective.”

The SUV behind them suddenly accelerated and closed the distance rapidly.

The driver leaned forward behind the wheel with the focused intensity of someone who had clearly decided that traffic regulations were a minor inconvenience.

“Oh,” Vernon said quietly.

“What?”

“That vehicle appears to be preparing to ram us again.”

The SUV slammed into the rear bumper.

The impact jolted the car violently.

Anzel tightened her grip on the steering wheel and corrected the skid with practiced control.

“That man,” she said calmly, “is becoming very annoying.”

Vernon watched the second SUV pull alongside them.

“Oh good,” he added. “They’ve brought additional unpleasantness.”

The passenger window of the SUV lowered slightly.

A man leaned out holding a compact firearm.

“Well,” Vernon said thoughtfully, “that seems excessive.”

The first shot shattered the side mirror.

Glass fragments scattered across the highway.

Several nearby drivers began swerving away in panic as horns erupted around them.

“Please tell me you have a plan,” Vernon said.

“I do.”

“That sounded dangerously confident.”

Anzel glanced ahead.

The highway curved toward a construction zone where traffic narrowed into two lanes between rows of orange safety barriers.

A large sign flashed:

ROADWORK AHEAD

“Perfect,” Anzel said.

Vernon followed her gaze.

“I am extremely concerned whenever you describe something like that as perfect.”

The SUV beside them fired again.

The bullet struck the rear quarter panel.

Anzel suddenly slammed the brakes.

The maneuver was so abrupt that the pursuing SUV overshot them slightly.

Then she accelerated again.

The car shot forward and squeezed between two construction barriers with only inches of clearance.

The SUV behind them attempted the same maneuver.

The driver clipped a barrier.

The vehicle fishtailed violently.

Vernon looked back just in time to watch the SUV slam sideways into a stack of traffic cones.

“Well,” he said calmly, “that seems like a professional setback.”

Unfortunately the second SUV was still very much operational.

It barreled through the narrowing construction lane directly behind them.

The gunman leaned out again.

Anzel exhaled slowly.

“That’s unfortunate.”

Vernon stared at the laptop screen.

“I am attempting to upload the Helix files to several media outlets but the connection keeps dropping.”

“Why?”

“They’re jamming signals.”

“That seems unfair.”

“They have significantly more resources than we do.”

Another shot rang out.

The bullet punched through the rear window.

Vernon ducked instinctively.

“Okay,” he said, “this situation has escalated beyond my preferred comfort level.”

Anzel suddenly swerved toward a massive construction truck parked along the roadside.

Vernon blinked.

“I do not believe driving toward heavy machinery improves our survival odds.”

“Trust me.”

“Historically those are the last words people hear before things go terribly wrong.”

The car roared past the truck.

Anzel reached out the window and grabbed the loose chain hanging from the truck’s rear cargo latch.

Vernon stared.

“I would very much like to know why you just stole construction equipment.”

“Physics,” Anzel replied calmly.

The chain snapped loose behind them and dragged across the asphalt like a metal whip.

The pursuing SUV slammed directly into it.

The chain tangled beneath the front wheels.

The vehicle skidded violently sideways.

Tires screeched.

The SUV spun across two lanes and collided with the highway divider in a shower of sparks.

Vernon watched the wreck through the rear window.

“Well,” he said slowly, “that was unexpectedly effective.”

Anzel finally eased off the accelerator.

Behind them traffic had devolved into chaotic confusion as drivers attempted to navigate around the wrecked SUVs and scattered construction equipment.

For the moment the pursuit had stopped.

Vernon leaned back in his seat and exhaled.

“I believe we may have temporarily discouraged them.”

“Temporarily,” Anzel agreed.

He looked down at the laptop screen again.

The upload progress bar crept slowly forward.

“What happens when they send more people?”

“They will.”

“Yes.”

“And probably something worse than SUVs.”

“Also yes.”

Vernon closed the laptop halfway.

“So our current plan is to expose a pharmaceutical conspiracy while being hunted by a multinational corporation.”

“That’s correct.”

He thought about that for a moment.

“You know,” he said carefully, “this day started with a printer problem.”

Anzel nodded.

“Yes.”

“And now we are fugitives.”

“Yes.”

“And someone just shot at us on a public highway.”

“Yes.”

Vernon sighed.

“I should have stayed home.”

Anzel glanced at him briefly.

“No you shouldn’t have.”

“Why not?”

“Because,” she said calmly, “now we know what they’re doing.”

Vernon looked back at the laptop screen where the Helix files continued uploading.

“And that makes us very dangerous to them.”

“Yes.”

He nodded slowly.

“Well,” Vernon said, “that explains the shooting.”

The Neverminded Protocol
Chapter 5: The Safe Place

The warehouse district was quiet at night in the way that suggested most people had decided long ago that nothing good ever happened there after sunset.

Anzel pulled the car into a narrow alley between two aging brick buildings and shut off the engine. The sudden silence felt strange after the chaos of the highway.

For several seconds neither of them moved.

Vernon finally exhaled slowly.

“I would like to officially state that I am having a very unusual day.”

Anzel leaned back in the seat.

“You hacked a biomedical corporation.”

“Yes.”

“You stole their research.”

“Yes.”

“And then we were chased down a highway by armed security vehicles.”

“Yes.”

Vernon nodded thoughtfully.

“Now that you summarize it like that, I realize this might actually qualify as a bad day.”

Anzel stepped out of the car and scanned the dark street before opening the rear door of the small building beside them.

“This place belongs to a friend,” she said.

“What kind of friend?”

“The kind who owes me several favors.”

“That sounds promising.”

They stepped inside.

The building was not a warehouse.

It was a workshop.

Electronic equipment covered nearly every surface. Workbenches were crowded with circuit boards, oscilloscopes, soldering tools, and computer towers that looked like they had been assembled from equal parts engineering skill and mild paranoia.

Vernon stopped in the doorway and stared.

“Oh,” he said softly.

“What?”

“This is beautiful.”

Anzel switched on the lights.

“I thought you might say that.”

The room brightened to reveal several large monitors mounted on the far wall along with a powerful workstation connected to what appeared to be an alarming number of network antennas.

Vernon walked toward the equipment slowly, like a man discovering a sacred temple.

“Who owns this place?”

“A former engineering student who became very successful fixing problems for people who prefer not to appear on official paperwork.”

Vernon nodded.

“Yes, that description makes sense.”

He placed the laptop on the central workstation and connected it to the system.

Multiple screens flickered to life.

The Helix files opened again, but now the processing power of the workshop computers began tearing through the encryption far faster than the laptop had managed earlier.

Anzel leaned against the workbench while Vernon scrolled through the documents.

Minutes passed.

The deeper he dug into the files, the more his expression changed.

“Anzel,” he said quietly.

“Yes.”

“This is worse than we thought.”

“That sentence is becoming very common today.”

He opened a large data set and expanded a series of maps.

Red markers appeared across several major cities.

Clinical trial zones.

Neurolift distribution points.

Population response tracking.

Anzel stepped closer.

“How many people?”

Vernon zoomed in.

“Approximately forty thousand.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“That’s not a test.”

“No.”

“That’s a deployment.”

“Yes.”

He opened another folder.

Corporate communications.

Government correspondence.

Contract negotiations.

Vernon read silently for a moment.

Then he leaned back slowly.

“Oh that’s very bad.”

“What?”

“They already have buyers.”

“Buyers?”

“Several governments.”

Anzel folded her arms.

“For crowd control?”

“For everything.”

He opened another document.

Emergency response projections.

Civil unrest scenarios.

Public compliance modeling.

If released across a population center the drug could suppress panic reactions for several hours.

During that time people would remain calm.

Passive.

Unresisting.

Anzel stared at the screen.

“That’s not medical research.”

“No.”

“That’s population control.”

“Yes.”

The room fell silent.

The scale of the plan hung heavily in the air.

Vernon opened one final folder.

Internal staff communications.

He began scanning the messages.

Then suddenly stopped.

“Wait.”

“What?”

“I recognize this name.”

He leaned closer to the monitor.

Anzel walked around the workstation.

“What name?”

Vernon pointed at the screen.

The message header displayed the sender clearly.

Dr. Elias Kade
Director of Neurological Development
Helix Biomedical

Anzel watched Vernon’s expression carefully.

“You know him.”

Vernon nodded slowly.

“Yes.”

“How?”

“We went to university together.”

“That seems unfortunate.”

Vernon kept staring at the screen.

“He was brilliant.”

“What happened?”

“He believed the human brain was fundamentally inefficient.”

“That sounds like the beginning of a villain speech.”

“He used to say fear and panic were evolutionary mistakes.”

Anzel crossed her arms.

“And now he’s trying to remove them.”

“Yes.”

The workstation speakers suddenly crackled.

Both of them looked up.

The monitors flickered.

A new window appeared on the central display.

A video call.

Incoming.

Vernon stared at it.

“That’s impossible.”

“What is?”

The connection activated.

The screen filled with the image of a man sitting in a sleek office somewhere far more expensive than the warehouse workshop.

He looked calm.

Intelligent.

Slightly amused.

Dr. Elias Kade smiled.

“Hello Vernon,” he said.

Anzel slowly reached for a metal wrench lying on the workbench.

Vernon stared at the screen.

“You’re tracking us.”

“Of course.”

Kade leaned back comfortably in his chair.

“I must admit I’m impressed. Breaking into Helix systems was never something I expected from you.”

Vernon folded his arms.

“You’re drugging entire cities.”

Kade shrugged.

“I’m improving them.”

Anzel stepped forward.

“That drug removes fear.”

“Yes.”

“And free will.”

“That’s a philosophical interpretation.”
