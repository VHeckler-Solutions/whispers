The Pharmacist Investigation – Book 1

Chapter 1 The Camera That Shouldn’t Have Worked

The pharmacy smelled faintly of antiseptic and peppermint toothpaste. The evening had settled into that quiet hour when the last customers had gone home and the streets outside were beginning to empty.

Vernon stood on a ladder near the ceiling, wrestling with a stubborn security camera and a bundle of wires that seemed determined to make his life difficult.

“Whoever installed this system clearly hated humanity,” he muttered.

Behind the counter, Anzel van der Berg leaned on her elbows and watched him with mild amusement.

“You’ve been saying that for fifteen minutes,” she said.

“Because it’s true.”

“You installed it.”

Vernon paused halfway through tightening a screw.

“That accusation feels unnecessary.”

Anzel smiled slightly and shook her head.

The monitor beside the counter flickered as the camera finally connected to the system. One by one the screens came alive, showing the quiet aisles of the pharmacy from different angles.

Shelves of medicine. Rows of vitamins. The empty front counter.

Everything looked perfectly normal.

Vernon climbed down from the ladder and walked over to the monitor to test the system.

“Let’s see if this thing actually works now.”

He tapped a few keys.

The footage rolled backward through earlier recordings from the night before.

Then Vernon frowned.

“Anzel… quick question.”

She looked up.

“Yes?”

“Do you ever come into the pharmacy at two in the morning?”

“No.”

“Do any of your staff?”

“No.”

Vernon slowly rotated the monitor toward her.

The screen showed a hooded figure entering the pharmacy at 2:13 AM.

The person moved calmly through the aisles, almost like they belonged there.

They didn’t go near the cash register.

They didn’t touch the narcotics cabinet.

Instead they stopped at the cold medicine shelf.

The figure took a bottle of cough syrup.

Then antihistamines.

Then eye drops.

Then a small box of beta blockers.

After that, they simply walked out of view.

The footage ended.

Vernon scratched his head.

“Well that’s the worst thief I’ve ever seen.”

But Anzel had already grabbed a pen.

She wrote the medications down carefully on a piece of paper.

Cough syrup. Antihistamines. Eye drops. Beta blockers.

Her expression slowly changed.

“That’s strange.”

Vernon leaned over her shoulder.

“What do those do?”

She looked at the list again.

“Individually? Almost nothing dangerous.”

Vernon waited.

Anzel folded the paper slowly.

“But together…”

He sighed.

“Please don’t say murder.”

Anzel looked up at him.

“Those drugs could stop someone’s heart.”

Silence filled the pharmacy.

Vernon stared at the screen for a long moment.

Then he leaned back against the counter.

“So what you’re telling me…”

“Yes.”

“…is that someone is building a murder weapon out of cough syrup and allergy medicine.”

Anzel nodded once.

“That appears to be exactly what’s happening.”

Vernon exhaled slowly.

“Well.”

He looked back at the monitor.

“That seems like the kind of problem we should absolutely investigate.”

Anzel sighed and rubbed her forehead.

“I already regret letting you fix that camera.”

Chapter 2 A Very Uncomfortable Discovery

The next morning the pharmacy smelled strongly of fresh coffee and antiseptic.

Vernon sat at the counter staring at the computer screen while rubbing his eyes like a man who had slept far less than he should have.

Anzel van der Berg walked in from the back room carrying two mugs.

“You look terrible,” she said calmly.

“Thank you,” Vernon replied.

“That wasn’t a compliment.”

She placed one of the mugs in front of him.

“You stayed up all night watching the footage again, didn’t you?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

Vernon rotated the monitor toward her.

“I ran the timestamps through the system.”

The screen showed several recordings from the previous week.

Each one showed the same hooded figure entering the pharmacy late at night.

Each time they took the same strange combination of medications.

Cold medicine.

Antihistamines.

Eye drops.

Beta blockers.

Then they calmly left.

Anzel folded her arms.

“So it wasn’t random.”

“No,” Vernon said.

“Definitely not random.”

He clicked to another screen.

“I also checked the pharmacy inventory logs.”

Anzel leaned closer.

“Please tell me you didn’t break the system.”

“I didn’t break it.”

He paused.

“I gently encouraged it to cooperate.”

She sighed.

“What did you find?”

Vernon pointed at the records.

“These medications have been disappearing for three weeks.”

Anzel frowned.

“But no one reported anything.”

“That’s because whoever is doing this is careful.”

He scrolled further down.

“They only take small amounts each time.”

She studied the list quietly.

Cold medicine.

Antihistamines.

Beta blockers.

Eye drops.

Her expression became more serious.

“That combination could absolutely cause cardiac failure.”

Vernon leaned back in his chair.

“Exactly.”

Anzel looked at him.

“So someone is poisoning people.”

“Yes.”

“With cough syrup.”

“Yes.”

She rubbed her temples.

“That is deeply disturbing.”

Vernon nodded.

“Now here’s the really uncomfortable part.”

He opened another tab.

“I checked pharmacies in nearby towns.”

Anzel blinked.

“You what?”

“Relax.”

“I only looked at publicly accessible inventory systems.”

“That still sounds illegal.”

“Technically.”

He pointed at the screen.

“Look.”

The same medications had been disappearing from pharmacies in two nearby towns.

Small quantities.

The same combination.

Anzel stared at the list.

“That can’t be a coincidence.”

“Nope.”

She leaned back slowly.

“So the thief is traveling.”

“Exactly.”

“And using multiple pharmacies so no one notices.”

“Correct.”

Silence filled the pharmacy for a moment.

Then Anzel asked quietly,

“Did anyone actually die?”

Vernon hesitated.

Then he opened a news article.

A small headline appeared on the screen.

LOCAL MAN DIES OF SUDDEN HEART ATTACK

The article described a healthy middle-aged man who had collapsed unexpectedly two days earlier.

Anzel read the details.

Her face slowly lost color.

“Those symptoms…”

“Yes,” Vernon said quietly.

“They match the drug interaction perfectly.”

Another silence followed.

Then Anzel looked at him.

“So we’re not imagining this.”

“No.”

She crossed her arms.

“That means there’s a murderer walking around using pharmacy shelves as a chemistry set.”

Vernon nodded once.

“Correct.”

She took a deep breath.

“Vernon.”

“Yes.”

“We should call the police.”

“That is an option.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“That is the correct option.”

Vernon leaned forward slightly.

“But…”

She closed her eyes.

“There’s a ‘but,’ isn’t there?”

“Yes.”

“What is it?”

He smiled slightly.

“If we call the police right now…”

“Yes?”

“…we’ll never find out how the killer is doing it.”

Anzel stared at him.

“You want to investigate a murderer.”

“I prefer the term carefully observe suspicious activity.”

“You’re unbelievable.”

“Probably.”

She walked to the pharmacy shelves and looked at the rows of medication.

Then she sighed.

“You realize this is a terrible idea.”

“Yes.”

“And dangerous.”

“Also yes.”

She turned back toward him.

“But if someone is experimenting with lethal drug combinations…”

Vernon nodded.

“…then people are going to die.”

“Yes.”

Another long pause.

Finally Anzel shook her head.

“I can’t believe I’m agreeing to this.”

Vernon grinned.

“So that’s a yes?”

She sighed.

“Yes.”

Then she pointed at him.

“But if we get arrested, I’m blaming you.”

“That seems fair.”

Chapter 3 The Worst Stakeout in History

The pharmacy closed at six.

By six fifteen Vernon had already eaten half the snacks.

Anzel van der Berg sat behind the counter watching him with the expression of someone who was beginning to question several recent life decisions.

“You were supposed to bring quiet stakeout food,” she said.

Vernon held up a greasy takeaway packet.

“This is quiet stakeout food.”

“It smells like fried onions and regret.”

“That’s the authentic stakeout experience.”

She sighed and leaned back in her chair.

Outside, the streetlights flickered on as evening settled over the town. The pharmacy sat in comfortable silence except for Vernon chewing loudly.

“You know,” Anzel said, “most people who suspect a murderer is using their pharmacy would call the police.”

Vernon nodded while eating.

“True.”

“And most pharmacists would also call the police.”

“Also true.”

“So remind me again why we’re hiding in the dark waiting for someone who might be poisoning people?”

Vernon swallowed and shrugged.

“Curiosity.”

“That’s not reassuring.”

He looked at the security monitor.

“Relax. The camera system will alert us if someone comes near the back door.”

Anzel crossed her arms.

“You said that with far too much confidence.”

Vernon grinned.

“That’s because I installed it.”

“That’s exactly why I’m worried.”

The minutes passed slowly.

Eight o’clock.

Nine o’clock.

Ten o’clock.

The pharmacy remained quiet.

Vernon leaned back in his chair.

“This might be the most boring investigation in history.”

Anzel rubbed her eyes.

“I told you this was a terrible idea.”

Vernon looked at the monitor again.

Nothing.

No movement.

Just empty aisles and shelves of medicine.

Anzel yawned.

“You said the thief usually comes around two in the morning.”

“Yes.”

“What time is it now?”

Vernon checked his watch.

“Ten thirty.”

She stared at him.

“So we’re planning to sit here for another three and a half hours?”

“Yes.”

She closed her eyes briefly.

“This is how I die.”

“You’re being dramatic.”

“Am I?”

Vernon opened another snack packet.

“Do you want some chips?”

“No.”

“You’re missing out.”

“On cholesterol?”

“On delicious cholesterol.”

The quiet grew heavier.

Midnight arrived.

The pharmacy lights were dimmed and the only sound was the faint hum of the refrigerator behind the counter.

Vernon blinked slowly.

Then again.

His eyelids felt heavy.

Across the counter Anzel rested her head on her arms.

“This is ridiculous,” she murmured.

“Just stay awake,” Vernon said.

“I am awake.”

Five minutes later she was asleep.

Ten minutes after that Vernon was asleep too.

The security cameras continued recording silently.

At 2:11 AM the back door opened.

A hooded figure stepped inside the pharmacy.

They moved calmly through the aisles.

Exactly as before.

Cold medicine.

Antihistamines.

Eye drops.

Beta blockers.

The figure paused briefly near the counter.

Vernon and Anzel slept peacefully less than three meters away.

The thief quietly placed something on the counter.

Then walked out.

The door closed.

The pharmacy returned to silence.

At 3:04 AM Vernon suddenly jerked awake.

He blinked in confusion.

“Oh no.”

Across from him Anzel stirred.

“What?”

He looked at the monitor.

The recording showed the hooded figure leaving the pharmacy nearly an hour earlier.

“We missed them,” he said.

Anzel sat up straight.

“You’re joking.”

“I wish I was.”

She stared at the screen.

“We fell asleep.”

“Yes.”

“During a stakeout.”

“Yes.”

She buried her face in her hands.

“This is the worst investigation ever.”

Vernon nodded slowly.

“That’s a fair assessment.”

Then he noticed something on the counter.

“Wait.”

Anzel looked up.

“What?”

He pointed.

“That wasn’t there before.”

Sitting on the counter was a small folded piece of paper.

Neither of them had seen it earlier.

Anzel carefully picked it up.

Inside was a printed receipt.

She read the header.

“Medical Supply Distribution Center.”

Vernon leaned closer.

“What did they buy?”

Anzel scanned the list.

Her eyes narrowed.

“These aren’t normal pharmacy supplies.”

“What are they?”

She looked up at him.

“Laboratory chemicals.”

Vernon slowly smiled.

“Well.”

He leaned back in his chair.

“That seems like a clue.”

Anzel sighed.

“Yes.”

Then she looked at the clock.

“It’s three in the morning.”

“And?”

“We just slept through the only person we were supposed to catch.”

“Also true.”

She folded the receipt carefully.

“But at least now we know where to start looking.”

Vernon grinned.

“See?”

She raised an eyebrow.

“What?”

“Successful investigation.”

Anzel stared at him.

“You’re unbelievable.”

“Probably.”

Chapter 4 The Clinic

The next morning Vernon felt like someone had replaced his brain with wet cardboard.

He sat in his car outside a small private medical clinic while staring at a takeaway coffee like it had personally offended him.

“This was your idea,” Anzel van der Berg said from the passenger seat.

“That’s not helpful right now.”

“You said it would be exciting.”

“I said it might be exciting.”

She held up the folded receipt they had found in the pharmacy.

“Medical Supply Distribution Center.”

Vernon nodded.

“And according to the supplier records, the last shipment went here.”

He pointed at the clinic across the street.

It was a small building with clean windows and a neat sign outside.

RIVERSIDE MEDICAL CLINIC

Nothing about it looked suspicious.

People walked in and out normally.

A nurse carried a clipboard.

A man in a suit walked through the front door.

Anzel folded her arms.

“This is a normal clinic.”

“Yes.”

“Normal doctors.”

“Yes.”

“Normal patients.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him.

“So remind me why we’re spying on them from a car like two incredibly bad detectives.”

Vernon sipped his coffee.

“Because the receipt says laboratory chemicals were delivered here.”

“That still doesn’t mean anything.”

“True.”

“So what exactly is the plan?”

Vernon thought for a moment.

“We go inside.”

“That’s not a plan.”

“We ask questions.”

“That’s also not a plan.”

“It’s the beginning of a plan.”

Anzel stared at him.

“You’re improvising.”

“Absolutely.”

She sighed deeply.

“This is how people end up on the evening news.”

“Relax.”

Vernon opened the car door.

“Let’s go meet some doctors.”

The clinic waiting room smelled strongly of disinfectant and cheap air freshener.

A television on the wall played a daytime talk show nobody was watching.

Anzel and Vernon sat quietly in two plastic chairs pretending to be normal patients.

Vernon leaned toward her.

“I feel suspicious.”

“That’s because you’re whispering.”

“I’m whispering because this is an investigation.”

“This is a waiting room.”

A nurse behind the desk looked at them briefly.

Anzel lowered her voice.

“Please try to look normal.”

“I am normal.”

“You’re staring at people like you’re solving a puzzle.”

“I might be.”

She closed her eyes briefly.

“Just act like a patient.”

Vernon nodded.

“Okay.”

Five seconds later he whispered again.

“That guy has a limp.”

“Stop observing people.”

“I can’t help it.”

“Try.”

The door to one of the examination rooms opened.

A tall man wearing a white coat stepped into the hallway.

He looked to be in his early forties with neat hair and an expression that suggested he took his job very seriously.

The nurse stood up.

“Doctor Mbeki, your next patient.”

The doctor nodded and glanced briefly around the waiting room.

His eyes paused on Anzel.

Then Vernon.

For just a moment.

Something about that look made Vernon uneasy.

Doctor Mbeki approached the desk.

“Any deliveries this morning?” he asked.

The nurse checked a clipboard.

“Yes, doctor. The laboratory shipment arrived earlier.”

Anzel’s eyes flicked toward Vernon.

Laboratory shipment.

Doctor Mbeki nodded.

“Good. Put it in the storage room.”

“Yes, doctor.”

He turned and walked down the hallway.

Vernon leaned toward Anzel again.

“That seems promising.”

She nodded slowly.

“Very promising.”

“So what now?”

Anzel stood up.

“We ask about the delivery.”

“You’re serious?”

“Yes.”

“That sounds incredibly suspicious.”

“I’m a pharmacist,” she said calmly.

“Asking about medical supplies is literally my job.”

Vernon blinked.

“That’s actually a good point.”

They approached the front desk.

The nurse looked up.

“Can I help you?”

Anzel smiled politely.

“Good morning. I’m a pharmacist and I noticed your clinic received a shipment from Medical Supply Distribution.”

The nurse nodded.

“Yes, that arrived earlier.”

“I was curious what kind of laboratory chemicals you ordered.”

The nurse hesitated slightly.

“I’m not sure I can discuss clinic orders.”

“That’s understandable,” Anzel said gently.

“We’ve had some issues with suppliers recently so I wanted to compare notes.”

The nurse seemed uncertain.

Just then Doctor Mbeki stepped back into the hallway.

“Is there a problem?” he asked.

Anzel turned toward him.

“No problem at all,” she said.

“I’m Anzel van der Berg. I run a pharmacy nearby.”

The doctor studied her for a moment.

Then he smiled politely.

“I’m Doctor Mbeki.”

Anzel held up the receipt.

“I was just asking about a delivery from Medical Supply Distribution.”

Doctor Mbeki’s smile remained.

But his eyes changed slightly.

Almost imperceptibly.

“We do occasionally order laboratory materials,” he said.

“For research purposes.”

Vernon spoke before thinking.

“What kind of research?”

Anzel shot him a warning look.

Doctor Mbeki tilted his head.

“Pharmaceutical toxicity studies.”

Vernon froze slightly.

“Toxicity.”

“Yes,” the doctor said calmly.

“Understanding how certain medications interact inside the human body.”

Anzel and Vernon exchanged a quick glance.

Drug interactions.

Exactly what they were investigating.

Doctor Mbeki continued watching them carefully.

“Why do you ask?”

Anzel smiled politely.

“Professional curiosity.”

The doctor nodded slowly.

“I see.”

There was a brief silence.

Then he spoke again.

“If you’re interested in pharmacology…”

His eyes lingered on Anzel.

“…perhaps we should talk further sometime.”

Vernon suddenly had a very uncomfortable feeling.

Something about this conversation felt wrong.

Very wrong.

And he had the strange suspicion that Doctor Mbeki might be studying them just as carefully as they were studying him.

Chapter 5 The Feeling of Being Watched

The moment they stepped outside the clinic, Vernon exhaled slowly.

“That man is dangerous.”

Anzel raised an eyebrow.

“You decided that in thirty seconds?”

“It was the way he looked at us.”

“That’s not evidence.”

“No,” Vernon said, unlocking his car.

“But it’s a very strong feeling.”

They climbed inside and closed the doors.

For a moment neither of them spoke.

Across the street the clinic doors opened again.

Doctor Mbeki stepped outside.

He stood near the entrance, speaking to the nurse.

But Vernon noticed something.

The doctor looked directly toward their car.

Not casually.

Deliberately.

“Don’t move,” Vernon muttered.

“I wasn’t planning to,” Anzel replied.

“Just look normal.”

“I am normal.”

“You’re staring again.”

“I’m observing.”

“That’s the same thing.”

Anzel sighed and leaned back in her seat.

“Fine. What exactly are you observing?”

Vernon kept his eyes forward.

“Doctor Mbeki is watching our car.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“Maybe he’s just looking outside.”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Because he’s pretending not to look.”

Anzel slowly turned her head just enough to see through the windshield.

Doctor Mbeki was still outside.

Still talking to the nurse.

Still occasionally glancing toward them.

Her voice lowered slightly.

“Alright.”

“See?”

“Yes.”

“That’s not normal.”

“No.”

They sat in silence for a moment.

Then Vernon started the engine.

“I think we should leave.”

“Agreed.”

The car pulled slowly away from the clinic.

Neither of them spoke until they turned the corner and the building disappeared behind them.

Finally Anzel said,

“That conversation was strange.”

“Extremely.”

“He knew exactly what we were asking about.”

“Yes.”

“And he immediately mentioned drug interactions.”

Vernon nodded.

“That’s not a coincidence.”

They stopped at a red traffic light.

Anzel stared out the window.

“You don’t think…”

She paused.

“What?”

“…that he might actually be the one doing it.”

Vernon drummed his fingers on the steering wheel.

“It’s possible.”

“But that would be incredibly risky.”

“Not if he understands the chemistry.”

Anzel looked back at him.

“You’re saying he could be creating poisons that look like natural medical events.”

“Exactly.”

The traffic light turned green.

They drove forward.

“Heart attacks,” Vernon continued.

“Strokes.”

“All caused by medication combinations that would look perfectly normal in a toxicology report.”

Anzel frowned.

“That would require an extremely deep understanding of pharmacology.”

“Yes.”

“And someone with access to laboratory chemicals.”

“Yes.”

“And someone confident enough to experiment on real people.”

They both said the next word at the same time.

“Doctor.”

Silence filled the car.

Then Anzel said quietly,

“If he is responsible…”

“Yes?”

“…then we might have just walked into the office of a murderer.”

Vernon nodded slowly.

“And introduced ourselves.”

They drove another block before Anzel spoke again.

“Vernon.”

“Yes.”

“If Doctor Mbeki is involved…”

“Yes?”

“…then he might already be suspicious of us.”

Vernon thought about the way the doctor had watched their car.

“I think he already is.”

Anzel looked uneasy.

“That’s not good.”

“No.”

She folded her arms.

“What do we do now?”

Vernon smiled slightly.

“We investigate.”

“That’s your solution to everything.”

“Yes.”

“And what does that investigation involve exactly?”

He glanced at her.

“Breaking into a clinic computer system.”

Anzel stared at him.

“You’re joking.”

“I wish I was.”

“You want to hack a medical clinic.”

“Technically we’re just accessing publicly available data.”

“That’s not what hacking means.”

“It’s a flexible definition.”

Anzel leaned back in her seat and sighed deeply.

“This investigation is getting out of control.”

“Probably.”

“And dangerous.”

“Also probably.”

She looked out the window.

“Tell me something honestly.”

“What?”

“If we keep digging…”

“Yes?”

“…are we going to regret it?”

Vernon thought about the footage.

The stolen medications.

Doctor Mbeki’s calm smile.

And the way his eyes had watched them.

“Almost definitely.”

Anzel closed her eyes briefly.

“Wonderful.”

Vernon turned the car toward the pharmacy.

“But we’re already involved now.”

“That’s the problem.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him.

“So when do we break into the clinic system?”

Vernon smiled.

“Tonight.”

Anzel groaned.

“I knew you were going to say that.”

Chapter 6 The Worst Break-In Plan Ever

At 11:47 PM Vernon parked the car three streets away from Riverside Medical Clinic.

The building was dark except for one dim security light near the back entrance.

Rain had started falling lightly.

Anzel stared at the clinic through the windshield.

“This is illegal.”

“Technically.”

“You said we were accessing publicly available data.”

“We are.”

She pointed at the backpack on the floor.

“That contains lock-picking tools.”

“Backup equipment.”

“And a laptop designed for breaking into networks.”

“Enhanced research tools.”

Anzel looked at him.

“You’re unbelievable.”

“Probably.”

She sighed.

“Tell me again why we’re doing this.”

Vernon grabbed the backpack.

“Because if Doctor Mbeki is experimenting with lethal drug combinations, more people are going to die.”

That answer stopped her from arguing.

After a moment she nodded.

“Fine.”

They stepped out into the rain.

The clinic parking lot was empty.

The only sound was water tapping against the pavement.

Vernon moved quietly toward the back door.

“Please tell me you actually know how to do this,” Anzel whispered.

“Of course.”

“How many times have you broken into a building before?”

Vernon paused.

“That’s a complicated question.”

“That’s not comforting.”

He knelt beside the door and pulled out a small set of tools.

Within twenty seconds the lock clicked open.

Anzel blinked.

“You’ve definitely done this before.”

“Let’s focus on the investigation.”

They slipped inside.

The clinic hallway was dark.

Emergency lights glowed faintly along the walls.

Everything smelled strongly of disinfectant.

Vernon whispered,

“Doctor’s office should be down this hallway.”

Anzel followed quietly.

“You seem very confident.”

“I checked the building layout earlier.”

“You studied the clinic floor plan?”

“Yes.”

“You’re enjoying this far too much.”

“Possibly.”

They reached a small office.

A nameplate on the door read:

Dr. Sipho Mbeki

Vernon opened the door slowly.

The office was neat and organized.

Desk.

Computer.

Medical charts.

Nothing looked suspicious.

“Alright,” Vernon said quietly.

“Let’s see what the good doctor has been researching.”

He placed the laptop on the desk and connected a small cable to the computer.

The screen flickered.

Lines of code began appearing.

Anzel leaned over his shoulder.

“I understand chemistry.”

“Yes.”

“But that looks like alien language.”

“Computer language.”

“That’s worse.”

Vernon typed quickly.

“Just accessing the local system…”

Files appeared on the screen.

Research logs.

Patient records.

Chemical orders.

Then Vernon stopped typing.

“Oh.”

Anzel looked at him.

“What?”

He opened a document.

A long list of chemical compounds filled the screen.

Beta blockers.

Antihistamines.

Decongestants.

Sedatives.

Anzel’s eyes widened.

“That’s the exact combination pattern.”

“Yes.”

Another document opened.

This one was worse.

A list of dates.

And next to each date…

A name.

Anzel read the first one.

“That’s the man who died last week.”

Vernon scrolled.

More names.

More dates.

More chemical combinations.

“This isn’t research,” she whispered.

“No.”

“This is a list of victims.”

Before Vernon could respond—

BEEP

Both of them froze.

“Did you touch something?” Anzel whispered.

“No.”

Another sound echoed through the building.

BEEP BEEP

Vernon looked at the screen.

“Oh no.”

“What?”

“I triggered the security system.”

“How?”

“I may have accessed the wrong server.”

The alarm suddenly screamed through the clinic.

WEEEEEOOOO WEEEEEOOOO

Anzel grabbed his arm.

“RUN.”

Vernon yanked the laptop cable free.

They sprinted down the hallway.

Lights began flashing.

Outside a distant siren started approaching.

“That’s not good,” Anzel shouted.

“No!”

They burst through the back door into the rain.

The parking lot lights turned on automatically.

Vernon slipped on the wet pavement but caught himself.

“THIS WAY!”

They ran across the lot and disappeared into the dark street.

Behind them the clinic alarm continued screaming.

After two blocks they finally stopped to catch their breath.

Anzel bent forward with her hands on her knees.

“That… was… a terrible plan.”

Vernon nodded.

“Fair.”

She looked up at him.

“But we saw the files.”

“Yes.”

“The names.”

“Yes.”

“That doctor is murdering people.”

Vernon wiped rain from his face.

“Now we just need to prove it.”

Anzel shook her head.

“You realize something.”

“What?”

“If he checks his system tomorrow…”

“Yes?”

“…he’ll know someone accessed those files.”

Vernon sighed.

“Correct.”

She stared at him.

“So now the killer knows someone is investigating him.”

“Yes.”

A long pause followed.

Then Anzel said quietly,

“This investigation just became very dangerous.”

Vernon nodded.

“Extremely.”

Chapter 7 The Watcher

The pharmacy was quiet that evening.

Too quiet.

Vernon sat in front of the security monitor with a cup of coffee and a feeling he couldn’t quite explain.

Across the counter, Anzel van der Berg was organizing medication shelves.

“You’ve been staring at that screen for ten minutes,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Are you expecting something to happen?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“I’m not sure.”

She looked at him.

“That’s not helpful.”

Vernon leaned closer to the monitor.

“Just a feeling.”

Anzel sighed.

“You and your feelings.”

“They’re usually right.”

“That’s debatable.”

Vernon clicked through the camera feeds.

Front entrance.

Empty street.

Back door.

Storage room.

Everything looked normal.

But something bothered him.

He rewound the footage from earlier that afternoon.

A man walked past the pharmacy window.

Nothing strange about that.

Then the same man walked past again.

Twenty minutes later.

Then again.

Thirty minutes after that.

Vernon leaned forward.

“That’s odd.”

Anzel looked over.

“What?”

He pointed at the screen.

“That guy.”

“What about him?”

“He’s walked past the pharmacy three times today.”

“Maybe he likes walking.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because he keeps looking inside.”

“He claims you’ve been stalking his clinic and interfering with his patients.”

Anzel looked shocked.

“That’s not true.”

“That may be.”

The officer’s voice remained calm.

“But from the evidence we have…”

He gestured toward the photograph.

“…it certainly looks that way.”

Vernon rubbed his face.

“So what happens now?”

Daniels looked directly at him.

“You come with us.”

The words hung in the air.

Anzel stepped forward immediately.

“Wait.”

“Yes?”

“You’re arresting him?”

“For unlawful access to medical systems.”

Vernon sighed.

“That sounds bad.”

Daniels reached for his handcuffs.

“Turn around.”

Anzel’s voice sharpened.

“You’re making a mistake.”

“Maybe.”

“But until you produce real evidence…”

The officer clicked the cuffs around Vernon’s wrists.

“…this investigation is over.”

Vernon looked at Anzel.

“Well.”

“That escalated quickly.”

Anzel’s mind raced.

Because she knew something Officer Daniels didn’t.

Doctor Mbeki wasn’t trying to stop the investigation.

He was trying to buy time.

And somewhere in the city…

The next victim was still waiting.

Chapter 15 The Calculation

The police station smelled like old coffee and paperwork.

Vernon sat at the small metal table staring at the wall clock.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

He rubbed his wrists where the handcuffs had been.

Officer Daniels stood nearby flipping through paperwork.

“You seem unusually calm,” Daniels said.

Vernon shrugged.

“I’ve had worse mornings.”

“That’s not comforting.”

“Life rarely is.”

Daniels closed the folder.

“You realize this situation is serious.”

“Yes.”

“You illegally accessed a medical clinic.”

“Yes.”

“And you’re accusing a respected doctor of murder.”

“Yes.”

Daniels sighed.

“Without evidence.”

“Working on that.”

Meanwhile…

Across town…

Anzel sat behind the pharmacy counter surrounded by medical textbooks.

The laptop screen glowed in front of her.

Patient records.

Drug interaction charts.

Metabolic pathways.

She whispered to herself.

“Think.”

The six victim profiles filled the screen.

Six conditions.

Six combinations.

Six deaths.

She began writing on a notepad.

Beta blockers.

Antihistamines.

ACE inhibitors.

Sedatives.

Each combination had been carefully adjusted.

“Not random,” she muttered.

“Designed.”

Her pencil moved faster.

Every victim had a unique medical vulnerability.

But the formula wasn’t just about the disease.

It was about metabolism.

She grabbed a pharmacology reference book.

“Cytochrome P450 enzymes…”

Her eyes widened.

“That’s it.”

She began typing quickly.

Each victim’s medication list shared something critical.

They all used drugs processed through the same metabolic pathway.

Doctor Mbeki wasn’t just mixing drugs randomly.

He was overloading a specific enzyme system.

The body couldn’t break the drugs down fast enough.

Toxic levels built silently.

Then the organs failed.

Heart.

Lungs.

Kidneys.

Each death looked like natural disease progression.

But it wasn’t.

It was engineered.

Anzel leaned back.

“Oh my god.”

Her phone buzzed suddenly.

Unknown number.

She answered.

“Hello?”

A calm voice replied.

“Miss van der Berg.”

Her blood ran cold.

“Doctor Mbeki.”

“I’m impressed,” he said.

“You figured it out.”

Anzel stood slowly.

“You’re murdering patients.”

“Such an emotional word.”

“You’re poisoning them.”

“I’m studying them.”

“You’re insane.”

Mbeki chuckled softly.

“On the contrary.”

“I’m very precise.”

Anzel’s voice hardened.

“We stopped you.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

“Mr. Kruger is alive.”

“For now.”

Her stomach tightened.

“What does that mean?”

Mbeki spoke calmly.

“You’re focusing on the wrong patient.”

Anzel froze.

“What?”

“Mr. Kruger was merely… a distraction.”

Cold dread filled her chest.

“If he’s not the next victim…”

“No.”

“Then who is?”

Mbeki’s voice remained perfectly calm.

“Someone much more interesting.”

Anzel felt the realization hit like a lightning strike.

“You.”

Mbeki laughed quietly.

“Not you, Miss van der Berg.”

He paused.

“I mean your friend.”

Her heart dropped.

“Vernon.”

“Yes.”

“While you were studying enzyme pathways…”

He spoke almost gently.

“…I was adjusting his medication.”

The phone slipped slightly in her hand.

“What did you do?”

Mbeki’s voice remained calm.

“Did you really think I would allow you to interfere with my research without consequences?”

Anzel grabbed her keys.

“If you’ve poisoned him—”

“Oh, I haven’t.”

“Yet.”

The line went silent.

Anzel ran out of the pharmacy.

Because suddenly the investigation was no longer about stopping the next victim.

It was about saving Vernon.

Chapter 16 The Experiment

Anzel drove faster than she ever had before.

The streets blurred past under the late afternoon sun.

Her mind replayed Mbeki’s words over and over.

“I was adjusting his medication.”

Her hands tightened on the steering wheel.

“No,” she muttered.

“Not happening.”

At the police station…

Vernon sat across from Officer Daniels again.

The room was quiet except for the hum of the overhead lights.

Daniels leaned back in his chair.

“You’re lucky.”

“Why?”

“The clinic hasn’t pressed full charges yet.”

“That’s generous.”

“Which means you’ll probably be released later today.”

Vernon nodded.

“That’s good news.”

Daniels studied him carefully.

“You still believe Doctor Mbeki is killing people.”

“Yes.”

“Even without evidence.”

“We’re getting there.”

Before Daniels could reply—

The police station door burst open.

Anzel rushed inside.

Both men turned.

“Vernon!”

Daniels stood immediately.

“Ma’am, you can’t just—”

She ignored him.

“Vernon we have a problem.”

“That seems to be a recurring theme.”

She grabbed his arm.

“Mbeki called me.”

The room went silent.

Daniels frowned.

“The doctor?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

Anzel looked directly at Vernon.

“He said he adjusted your medication.”

Vernon blinked.

“What medication?”

“The antihistamines you took last week.”

Realization hit.

“Oh.”

Daniels looked confused.

“Someone want to explain?”

Anzel spoke quickly.

“Mbeki’s experiments involve enzyme overload.”

Daniels raised an eyebrow.

“English, please.”

She pointed at Vernon.

“If he changed Vernon’s prescription…”

“Yes?”

“…he may have triggered the same interaction pattern.”

Daniels’ expression shifted.

“You’re saying the doctor poisoned him.”

“Yes.”

Vernon leaned back.

“That’s concerning.”

Daniels stood.

“Do you feel sick?”

“No.”

“Dizzy?”

“No.”

“Shortness of breath?”

“No.”

Anzel checked her watch.

“When did you take the medication?”

“Yesterday morning.”

Her stomach dropped.

“That means the metabolic cascade could start anytime.”

Daniels stared at her.

“You’re serious.”

“Yes.”

He grabbed the phone.

“I’m calling an ambulance.”

“No,” Anzel said.

“Why not?”

“Because we need proof.”

Daniels looked stunned.

“Your friend might be dying and you want proof?”

“Yes.”

She pointed toward the door.

“Mbeki designed this experiment.”

“So?”

“So he’ll want to watch the result.”

Vernon nodded slowly.

“That sounds like him.”

Daniels folded his arms.

“You’re suggesting the doctor will show up somewhere.”

“Yes.”

“To observe the experiment.”

“Yes.”

Silence filled the room.

Finally Daniels sighed.

“This is the most ridiculous investigation I’ve ever heard.”

Anzel met his eyes.

“Maybe.”

“But if we’re right…”

“…we can catch him in the act.”

Daniels stared at Vernon.

“You’re willing to risk this?”

Vernon shrugged.

“I’ve had worse days.”

Anzel grabbed her bag.

“We go to the clinic.”

“Why the clinic?” Daniels asked.

“Because that’s where he records his results.”

Daniels exhaled slowly.

“This is insane.”

“Yes,” Vernon said.

“But if it works…”

He stood up.

“…we finally end this.”

Chapter 17 The Observation

The clinic was quiet when they arrived.

Too quiet.

The evening sky had turned dark blue as the streetlights flickered on.

Officer Daniels parked the car across the street.

“You’re certain he’ll come here?” he asked.

Anzel nodded.

“This is where he stores the research logs.”

“And where he watches the results.”

Vernon leaned back in the seat.

“So this is the observation room.”

Daniels glanced at him.

“You’re making jokes.”

“It’s a coping mechanism.”

“Do you feel anything yet?”

Vernon thought for a moment.

“Maybe.”

Anzel turned quickly.

“What do you mean maybe?”

“A little dizzy.”

Her face went pale.

“That’s not good.”

Daniels reached for the radio.

“We should get him to a hospital.”

“No,” Anzel said.

“Not yet.”

“You’re gambling with his life.”

“Yes.”

She looked at Vernon.

“How bad is it?”

“Like standing up too fast.”

“That could escalate.”

“Probably.”

Daniels shook his head.

“This is insane.”

Before anyone could reply—

The clinic door opened.

All three of them froze.

Doctor Mbeki stepped outside.

He looked calm.

Almost relaxed.

As if he had simply come outside for fresh air.

Daniels whispered,

“That’s him.”

Anzel nodded.

“Yes.”

Mbeki walked slowly across the parking lot.

Then he stopped.

And looked directly at their car.

A faint smile appeared.

“He knows we’re here,” Vernon said.

“Of course he does,” Anzel replied.

Mbeki walked toward them.

Unhurried.

Confident.

When he reached the car window he knocked lightly.

Daniels lowered the window slowly.

“Doctor.”

“Officer.”

Mbeki’s eyes moved calmly between them.

“Miss van der Berg.”

“Mr. Vernon.”

Vernon gave a weak wave.

“Hello.”

Mbeki smiled slightly.

“I assume you’ve figured out the experiment.”

Anzel’s voice hardened.

“You poisoned him.”

“Such a harsh description.”

“You altered his medication.”

“Yes.”

Daniels stepped out of the car.

“Doctor Mbeki, I need you to explain exactly what you’ve done.”

Mbeki sighed.

“You’re interrupting a very important observation.”

Daniels’ voice sharpened.

“This isn’t a laboratory.”

“No,” Mbeki said.

“It’s much better.”

He looked at Vernon.

“How are you feeling?”

“Lightheaded.”

“Excellent.”

Anzel clenched her fists.

“You’re insane.”

“No.”

Mbeki looked almost offended.

“I’m curious.”

He gestured calmly.

“You see, Mr. Vernon represents a very interesting metabolic profile.”

Daniels stepped forward.

“That’s enough.”

Mbeki ignored him.

“I adjusted the dosage to trigger a delayed interaction.”

He checked his watch.

“Symptoms should begin progressing soon.”

Vernon leaned against the car door.

“That sounds bad.”

“Yes,” Mbeki said calmly.

“It will be fascinating.”

Daniels grabbed the doctor’s arm.

“You’re under arrest.”

Mbeki laughed softly.

“For what?”

“Attempted murder.”

“Do you have proof?”

Anzel stepped forward.

“Yes.”

She held up her phone.

The recording was still running.

Every word.

Every confession.

Mbeki’s smile faded.

“Ah.”

Daniels tightened his grip.

“That’s the proof.”

Mbeki looked disappointed.

“Well.”

“That’s unfortunate.”

Then Vernon suddenly stumbled.

His vision blurred.

The world tilted.

Anzel caught him.

“Vernon!”

His breathing became shallow.

Mbeki watched calmly.

“Ah.”

“The reaction is accelerating.”

Daniels shouted into his radio.

“Ambulance. Now.”

Anzel glared at Mbeki.

“If he dies—”

Mbeki shrugged.

“Then the data will still be valuable.”

Chapter 18 The Aftermath

The ambulance lights painted the street in flashing red and blue.

Paramedics moved quickly.

“Pulse weak.” “Blood pressure dropping.” “Get oxygen on him.”

Vernon barely heard the voices around him.

The world felt distant.

Blurred.

Somewhere nearby Anzel was shouting.

“He altered the medication.”

“We know,” a paramedic replied.

“What drug interaction?”

“Multiple metabolic inhibitors.”

They lifted Vernon onto the stretcher.

The cold night air hit his face as they rushed him into the ambulance.

Inside the vehicle machines began beeping.

A paramedic inserted an IV line.

“Antihistamine overload mixed with beta blockers,” Anzel said quickly.

The paramedic looked at her.

“You a doctor?”

“Pharmacist.”

“Good.”

He handed her a medical kit.

“Help us stabilize him.”

Meanwhile outside the clinic…

Officer Daniels pushed Doctor Mbeki against the police car.

“You’re under arrest for attempted murder.”

Mbeki sighed.

“You’re overreacting.”

“You poisoned him.”

“I conducted a study.”

Daniels tightened the handcuffs.

“You’re done studying.”

Mbeki looked toward the ambulance calmly.

“Pity.”

“What?”

“He was a fascinating subject.”

Daniels shook his head.

“You’re a psychopath.”

“No.”

Mbeki replied calmly.

“I’m curious.”

Inside the ambulance…

Vernon slowly opened his eyes.

Everything felt heavy.

Machines beeped around him.

Anzel leaned over him.

“You’re still alive.”

“That’s good.”

“Yes.”

He blinked slowly.

“Did we catch him?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He paused.

Then smiled weakly.

“So technically… we solved the case.”

Anzel shook her head.

“You nearly died.”

“But we solved it.”

She sighed.

“You’re impossible.”

“Probably.”

He looked at her.

“Thank you.”

“For what?”

“For not giving up.”

Anzel smiled faintly.

“You’re welcome.”

The ambulance doors closed.

Sirens screamed as it sped toward the hospital.

Two weeks later…

The pharmacy was quiet again.

Sunlight streamed through the front windows.

Vernon sat behind the counter drinking coffee.

Anzel organized medications on the shelves.

“So,” Vernon said.

“Yes?”

“Doctor Mbeki is facing multiple murder charges.”

“Yes.”

“His assistant testified.”

“Yes.”

“And the police recovered his research files.”

“Yes.”

Vernon nodded.

“So that’s the end.”

Anzel smiled slightly.

“I hope so.”

The doorbell rang as a customer entered.

Normal life returning.

Vernon leaned back in his chair.

“You know something.”

“What?”

“This whole situation started because of curiosity.”

Anzel looked at him.

“Yes.”

“And curiosity almost killed us.”

“Also yes.”

Vernon smiled.

“Worth it.”

She laughed quietly.

“You’re unbelievable.”

“Probably.”

The pharmacy door opened again.

Officer Daniels stepped inside.

“Morning.”

“Morning,” Vernon replied.

Daniels placed a newspaper on the counter.

The headline read:

LOCAL DOCTOR ARRESTED IN MEDICAL MURDER CASE

“Looks like you were right,” Daniels said.

Anzel smiled.

“Sometimes curiosity pays off.”

Daniels looked at Vernon.

“Just try not to break into any more clinics.”

“No promises.”

Daniels sighed.

“I was afraid you’d say that.”
